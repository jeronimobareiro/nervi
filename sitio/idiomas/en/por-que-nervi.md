<p class="nv-antetitulo">The name</p>

<p class="nv-titulo-grande">Why is it called Nervi?</p>

Nervi is named after **Pier Luigi Nervi** (Sondrio, 1891 – Rome, 1979), the Italian engineer who showed that a well-calculated structure can be, at the same time, economical, easy to build and beautiful.

In Italian, *nervi* means "nerves", and the word is related to *nervature*: the ribs that run through his slabs and domes, following the path along which forces travel. We chose his name because it sums up what we want this program to be: **rigorous calculation in the service of those who design and build.**

</div>
</div>

## Who he was

<div class="nv-columnas" markdown>

<div markdown>

Nervi graduated as a civil engineer from the University of Bologna in 1913. After serving in the army's engineering corps during the First World War, he founded his own construction company in Rome. That set him apart from almost all of his colleagues: he not only calculated structures, he also built them, and he knew first-hand the cost of every piece of formwork and every cubic metre of concrete.

His first known work was the Florence stadium (1929–1932), with a cantilevered canopy and helical concrete staircases that seem to float. Then came the aircraft hangars of the 1930s, long-span roofs made of a grid of crossing ribs; those of the second series were already assembled from prefabricated pieces. The hangars were destroyed during the Second World War, but the idea of prefabricating the structure remained one of his great contributions.

</div>

<div markdown>

In the 1940s he developed **ferrocement**: layers of fine steel mesh covered with mortar, which allow thin, light and strong pieces without the need for large timber formwork. With it he built everything from a sailboat to the undulating vaults of Torino Esposizioni.

From the 1950s his work crossed borders: the UNESCO headquarters in Paris, with Marcel Breuer and Bernard Zehrfuss; a tower in Montreal; a cathedral in San Francisco.

He taught at the Faculty of Architecture in Rome, wrote books such as *Scienza o arte del costruire?* (1945) and *Costruire correttamente* (1955), and gave the Charles Eliot Norton lectures at Harvard University (1961–1962). Among other honours, he received the Gold Medal of the Royal Institute of British Architects (1960) and that of the American Institute of Architects (1964).

</div>

## How he thought

For Nervi, calculating, building and giving form were one and the same task. A correct structure, he said, is not one that resists through sheer quantity of material, but one that understands how loads flow and puts the material exactly where it is needed.

<div class="nv-destacado" markdown>

The best example is the Gatti wool factory in Rome (1951–1953). The ribs of its slabs do not form a grid: they follow the **isostatic lines**, the paths of the principal stresses that come out of the calculation. The result is a more efficient slab and, at the same time, a ceiling of unexpected beauty. It is the form of the structure made visible.

Those lines are exactly what a finite element program calculates today. Nervi had to obtain them with hand calculation methods and scale models; we want any student, teacher or engineer to be able to see them on their computer, for free and with the code in plain view.

</div>

<p class="nv-titulo-grande">What he leaves us</p>

Nervi never separated theory from the work. He calculated in order to build better, and built in order to check what he had calculated. He believed that good engineering is born from understanding the structure, not from copying recipes.

Nervi, the program, wants to follow that path: calculation that is transparent and verifiable, so that anyone can learn how it reaches each number, and knowledge that belongs to everyone.

**If you study, teach or practise engineering, this project is yours too.** You can start with a verification case from a textbook, a coursework assignment or a thesis.
